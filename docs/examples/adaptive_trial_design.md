## Overview

FDA does not have a single oncology-specific adaptive design guidance; instead, oncology sponsors draw on 
a cross-cutting adaptive design guidance plus several oncology-specific documents on trial structure and 
dose selection.

## The core adaptive design guidance

The foundational document is *Adaptive Design Clinical Trials for Drugs and Biologics*, issued by CDER and
CBER in December 2019 under docket FDA-2018-D-3124. It describes important principles for designing, 
conducting, and reporting adaptive clinical trials, and advises on what information to submit for FDA 
evaluation, including for Bayesian adaptive designs and complex trials that rely on simulation. The 
recommendations apply to sponsors and applicants submitting INDs, NDAs, BLAs, or supplemental 
applications. In finalizing the guidance, FDA reworked the subsection on Bayesian adaptive designs and 
clarified the extent of prespecification expected. A secondary summary indicates FDA frames four key 
principles, the first being that the chance of erroneous conclusions must be controlled.

**Submission and interaction expectations.** All important information should reach FDA during the design 
stage, even if spread across different documents, so the Agency has a sufficient basis for review. 
Simulation reports should describe the parameter configurations used for each scenario with a 
justification of their adequacy, and state the number of simulated iterations per scenario along with a 
rationale for that number. Interim meetings should be documented with written minutes recording what was 
reviewed, discussed, and decided, and a marketing application relying on an adaptive trial should contain 
enough information and documentation for FDA to evaluate it. Because special protocol assessments carry a 
45-day response timeline, FDA recommends using them for complex adaptive designs only after extensive 
prior discussion with the Agency. One comparison document suggests FDA encourages sponsors to raise 
adaptive options at End-of-Phase-2 and Type C meetings, with greater design complexity warranting earlier 
interaction.

**Operational mechanics.** Interim unblinded data are typically reviewed by an independent statistician or
an independent DSMB applying pre-specified decision rules. Sample size re-estimation recalculates the 
target sample size partway through using either blinded information — such as observed variability or 
event rate without unblinding assignment — or unblinded information.

## Oncology-specific designs

*Master Protocols: Efficient Clinical Trial Design Strategies to Expedite Development of Oncology Drugs 
and Biologics* (March 2022) covers trials evaluating more than one investigational drug and/or more than 
one cancer type within a single overall structure, in adult and pediatric cancers. Unlike traditional 
designs testing one drug in one population per trial, master protocols use a single infrastructure, trial 
design, and protocol. FDA generally expects the recommended phase 2 dose to have been established for 
drugs evaluated in a master protocol, and the final version added material on dose-finding or safety 
lead-in cohorts. The final guidance's availability was announced March 2, 2022.

*Expansion Cohorts: Use in First-In-Human Clinical Trials to Expedite Development of Oncology Drugs and 
Biologics* was finalized in March 2022, following an August 2018 draft from CDER/CBER/OCE, under docket 
FDA-2018-D-2777. Commentary on that guidance notes the importance of carefully assessing the safety 
profile of each drug when evaluating combinations, and that FDARA 2017's pediatric evaluation requirement 
for molecularly targeted oncology drugs supports including pediatric expansion cohorts in first-in-human 
trials.

## Dose optimization (Project Optimus)

Project Optimus is an OCE initiative to reform the dose optimization and dose selection paradigm in 
oncology, aiming to educate, innovate, and collaborate with companies, academia, professional societies, 
international regulators, and patients. FDA communicates its dose-finding expectations through guidance, 
workshops, and public meetings, and encourages developers to meet with oncology staff.

The anchoring document, *Optimizing the Dosage of Human Prescription Drugs and Biological Products for the
Treatment of Oncologic Diseases*, was finalized in August 2024, following a January 2023 draft, with 
availability announced August 9, 2024. Some of its principles may extend to other therapeutic modalities 
or to pediatric dosage optimization. The recommendations respond to the conventional paradigm in which a 
maximum tolerated dose from initial dose-finding is carried forward into approval-supporting trials — a 
methodology developed for cytotoxic chemotherapy on the premise that efficacy and toxicity rise in 
parallel. Practical mechanisms discussed include dose comparison expansion cohorts studying similar 
populations across multiple potentially optimized doses at the end of escalation, and leveraging data from
other therapies of the same or similar class or mechanism to support the dosages selected. If premarket 
review finds signals that the optimal dose was not identified — for example, high rates of dose 
modification or discontinuation — post-marketing requirements may follow. Notably, a draft statement 
calling for enrollment of an appropriately broad population to assess dosages across subpopulations was 
omitted from the final version. Related guidance on oncology therapeutic radiopharmaceuticals directs 
readers to consider the August 2024 dosage guidance alongside it.

## Adjacent and emerging guidance

*Interacting with the FDA on Complex Innovative Trial Designs for Drugs and Biological Products* (December
2020, docket FDA-2019-D-3679) is the companion document for engaging FDA on novel designs. A draft ICH 
guidance, *E20 Adaptive Designs for Clinical Trials*, was issued in September 2025 under docket 
FDA-2025-D-3023. OCE also publishes a Guidance Agenda signaling planned new and updated documents.
