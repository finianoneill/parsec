## The short answer

FDA does not have a single "adaptive design in oncology" guidance; instead, oncology sponsors work from a 
general adaptive design guidance plus several oncology-specific design and dose-optimization documents.

## The foundational document

The core document is *Adaptive Design Clinical Trials for Drugs and Biologics: Guidance for Industry*, 
dated December 2019 (Docket FDA-2018-D-3124), issued jointly by CDER and CBER. It was announced in the 
Federal Register on December 2, 2019 (84 FR 65983-65985) and finalized an earlier draft, with its primary 
focus on adaptive designs for trials intended to support effectiveness and safety.

An adaptive design is defined as one that allows prospectively planned modifications to one or more 
aspects of the trial based on accumulating data from subjects already enrolled. The defining feature is 
prospective planning: the possible changes, the triggering data, and the decision rules are specified 
before the trial starts. Key themes include a clear pre-specified adaptation plan, adequate control of the
overall Type I error rate across all planned adaptations and analyses, and maintenance of trial integrity.
Estimation bias is also a concern — a conventional end-of-trial effect estimate such as a sample mean that
ignores the adaptations would tend to overestimate the true treatment effect.

On process, all important information should be submitted to FDA during the design stage so the agency has
enough to evaluate the proposed design. Simulation expectations are explicit: the parameters used for 
simulation scenarios with justification of their adequacy, and the number of simulated trials (iterations)
per scenario with a rationale. At the marketing-application stage, a trial with an adaptive design should 
be documented sufficiently for thorough FDA review, including all prospective plans and relevant committee
charters. Special protocol assessments, given the 45-day response timeline, are recommended for complex 
adaptive designs only where there has been extensive prior discussion with FDA.

A commentary comparing the guidance with ICH E20 indicates it covers limiting erroneous conclusions (Type 
I error probability, impact on safety and secondary endpoints), trial integrity through limiting knowledge
of unblinded data, independent IDMCs, confidentiality agreements and documented processes, adaptation 
types such as treatment selection and response- or covariate-adaptive randomization, Bayesian methods 
including borrowing of external information and priors, and use of adaptations in early-phase exploratory 
trials. That same source suggests FDA expects a rationale, a detailed adaptation plan (number and timing 
of analyses, modifications, decision rule) and defined roles for the DMC or adaptation committee before 
the trial, and encourages discussion at End-of-Phase-2 or Type C meetings, with earlier interaction as 
complexity increases.

## Oncology-specific design guidances

*Master Protocols: Efficient Clinical Trial Design Strategies to Expedite Development of Oncology Drugs 
and Biologics* was issued in March 2022 and announced in the Federal Register of March 2, 2022 (87 FR 
11722-11724). In contrast to traditional designs testing a single drug in a single population, master 
protocols use a single infrastructure, trial design, and protocol. The guidance generally expects the 
recommended phase 2 dose to have been established for drugs evaluated in a master protocol, and the final 
version added information about a dose-finding or safety lead-in cohort.

*Expansion Cohorts: Use in First-In-Human Clinical Trials to Expedite Development of Oncology Drugs and 
Biologics*, also March 2022 (87 FR 11720-11722), addresses the characteristics of drug products best 
suited for development under a multiple expansion cohort trial, with the final version clarifying the 
suitability criteria.

For novel designs generally, *Interacting with the FDA on Complex Innovative Trial Designs for Drugs and 
Biological Products* (December 2020, Docket FDA-2019-D-3679) governs sponsor–agency engagement.

## Dose optimization: the oncology overlay

Project Optimus, an Oncology Center of Excellence initiative, is reforming the dose optimization and dose 
selection paradigm in oncology development. Its stated goal is to educate, innovate, and collaborate with 
companies, academia, professional societies, international regulators, and patients, and its activities 
include communicating dose-finding expectations through guidance, workshops, and public meetings while 
encouraging developers to meet with FDA Oncology. The associated guidance, *Optimizing the Dosage of Human
Prescription Drugs and Biological Products for the Treatment of Oncologic Diseases*, was issued in draft 
in January 2023 and finalized in August 2024.

The practical implications for trial design were explored at a February 2024 FDA–AACR workshop on 
quantitative approaches to dosage optimization. Oncology dosage selection has traditionally relied on 
dose-finding trials to determine a maximum tolerated dose that is then carried into approval-supporting 
trials, and establishing the MTD remains important for defining the safe upper limit of dosing. Beyond 
classic escalation, controlled backfill and expansion cohorts can build understanding of dose–exposure 
relationships for safety and activity, with backfill design driven by the number of dosages backfilled to,
population variability, observed activity, and biomarker data. At the end of escalation, dose comparison 
expansion cohorts in similar populations across multiple candidate doses are one way to compare their 
merits. Because early oncology studies use small numbers of patients per dose level, modeling all early 
data to select doses for further evaluation matters; efficacy and safety are usually analyzed separately 
and compared qualitatively, and quantitative integrative approaches such as clinical utility indices 
combining safety and efficacy into a single index are advocated.

The consequence of getting this wrong is concrete: insufficiently characterized therapies can reach 
approval at a suboptimal dose and schedule, prompting postmarketing requirements, which are a fallback 
rather than a substitute for earlier optimization. In one example, FDA noted "limited dose finding" in the
selinexor Phase 1 study at an ODAC meeting and cited gastrointestinal toxicity from a high oral dose and 
pill burden as grounds for investigating a lower dosage. Lower-grade symptomatic adverse events such as 
diarrhea, which affect daily life and can prevent extended therapy, warrant monitoring in dose 
optimization.

## Watching for updates

FDA's Oncology Center of Excellence maintains a Guidance Agenda listing potential topics for new and 
updated oncology guidances, with comments referenced to Docket FDA-2024-N-5352.

## Gaps in this summary

This answer is assembled from guidance landing pages, Federal Register notices, and secondary commentary 
rather than full guidance text, so several areas remain incompletely verified. Specifically, a complete 
inventory of current, draft, and withdrawn guidances and adjacent initiatives (Project FrontRunner, the 
CID Paired Meeting Program, Real-Time Oncology Review) was not completed; the full internal detail of the 
2019 adaptive design guidance was only partially retrieved; the master protocols guidance's treatment of 
umbrella, basket, and platform trial mechanics and seamless Phase 1/2/3 designs was not extracted; and the
specific interaction between the 2024 dose-optimization guidance's randomized dose-ranging expectations 
and adaptive design choices was not directly confirmed. Readers making regulatory decisions should consult
the primary guidance texts and engage FDA directly.
